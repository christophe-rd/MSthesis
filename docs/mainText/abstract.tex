\chapter{Abstract}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Shifted phenology with ACC extends GS; expected growth increase
\item Recent studies failed to find this relationship; with drought and competition restraining growth 
\item Objective: understand if trees grow more with longer GS where drought and competition were limited
\item Leveraging 9 years of phenology from 2 studies, both in an arboretum and by relating it to tree growth
\item Juveniles grew more; adults did not change their growth with longer GS
\item Our results reveal a more complex growth response to climate change than currently assumed
\item Not massive responses and when they do, it's more for juveniles than mature: good for forest regeneration, potentially bad for forest carbon storage.
\end{enumerate}

Anthropogenic climate change affects many natural systems at the global scale. The most frequently observed biological impact of climate change---shifts in the timing of recurring life history events (phenology)---is likely to have cascading/additional/knock-on effects. For trees, shifted phenology has extended the vegetative growing season, which is widely expected to increase tree growth, with important effects on forest carbon sequestration dynamics. However, multiple recent studies have failed to find this relationship and suggested shifts in drought or competition may prevent increased growth. Here, we leverage two unique datasets of vegetative phenology and growth (tree rings) data, one from a common garden of four species of juvenile trees and the other from a citizen science program with eleven species of mature trees. Both of these projects took place in an urban arboretum where drought and competition are limited. We found an overall positive response of longer and warmer seasons for our juvenile trees, with little effect of provenance. Most of our species showed a stronger response to the end of season than the start of season, suggesting we should look beyond just the start of season when studying growth responses to longer growing season. However, our second study on mature trees coroborate recent results on mature trees. Our results show a weak response to longer and warmer seasons and life-history strategies did not matter. We showed that considering more than one year of growing conditions may reveal hidden postive growth trend and highlight the need to better our understanding of carbon allocation to storage and growth. These responses suggest that longer seasons with climate change may benefit forest regeneration and range expansion, but negatively impact overall carbon storage of temperate forests.



Shifted phenology with climate change has extended the vegetative growing season, which is widely expected
to increase tree growth. Multiple recent studies, however, have failed to find this relationship and suggested
shifts in drought or competition may prevent increased growth. Here, we ask if trees grow more with longer
seasons in an environment where drought and competition are limited. We aim to answer this question by
leveraging 9 years of vegetative phenology data from two studies located in an urban arboretum and by
relating it to tree growth (Figure 1). We found contrasting evidence that trees grow more during longer
seasons. Nine of our species did not change their growth; one grew less at its adult stage but more at its
juvenile stage, and two additional species grew more with longer seasons. These responses increased when
using the calendar instead of the thermal growing season. Our main findings demonstrate that tree growth
responses with climate change may be more complex than previously suggested. The shifts in response to
season lengths were not massive, and when they happened it was from juvenile more than mature trees.
